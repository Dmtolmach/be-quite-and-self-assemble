What is being simulated

Particles and potentials

Each amphiphile is an A–B dumbbell held by a harmonic bond. Every bead feels a Lennard-Jones potential, cut and shifted to zero at rc.

U_LJ(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶] − U_LJ(r_c) U_bond(r) = ½ k (r − r₀)², k = 200

Only the tail–tail pair (and optionally head–solvent) keeps the attractive well, with rc = 2.5σ. All other pairs are purely repulsive (WCA, rc = 21/6σ). The solvophobic drive is the B–B attraction against an athermal solvent. Number density is 0.6 in every system.

What to look for

Start at T* = 2.0: molecules are dispersed. Cool to 0.5: tails find each other and small micelles grow over tens of τ. Heat back to 2.0: micelles fall apart within a few τ. Breakup is fast and assembly is slow, because assembly needs diffusion and collisions.

At fixed T* = 0.5, lower the concentration to about 8%: most molecules stay free. Raise it to 50%: most join micelles. Ideal micelle theory says the free-dumbbell density levels off near the critical micelle concentration; allow 100 τ or more per concentration to test that. Lowering εBB acts like heating. A micelle is any cluster of at least 4 molecules whose tails are within 1.5σ.

Particles and potentials

Each chain is Nc P beads (3–40, 12 by default) joined by harmonic bonds, with a bending penalty that favours straight chains. P–P pairs attract with well depth εPP (rc = 2.5σ). P–S and S–S pairs are purely repulsive, so the solvent is poor for the polymer.

U_bend(α) = k_bend (1 − cos α) α = angle between consecutive bonds

Defaults εPP = 1.75 and kbend = 6.4 were calibrated for Nc = 12 at 40% polymer; other lengths shift the crossover.

What to look for

At T* = 2.2 the chains form a disordered polymer liquid. Cool to 1.2: within tens of τ, segments align side by side into small hexagonally packed crystallites (gold). Heat to 2.0: they melt within about 10 τ. Crystallinity stays partial (about 20% after 75 τ at 40% polymer, about 30% at 80%), as in real semicrystalline polymers.

A bead is crystalline when its six-fold order |ψ6| exceeds 0.6 and agrees in phase with at least 4 polymer neighbours within 1.45σ, inside a crystallite of 10 or more beads.

Particles and potentials

Each chain is a block of A beads followed by a block of B beads (Nc = 16, fA = 0.5 by default). All pairs attract with ε = 1, so the solvent is equally good for both blocks, except A–B, which attracts more weakly (εAB = 0.2). The missing A–B attraction is the incompatibility that χ describes in Flory–Huggins theory, with χ ∝ (1 − εAB)/T.

Because the blocks are bonded, they cannot separate into two large phases. They form domains about one chain size wide instead: microphase separation.

What to look for

Cool from 2.2 to 0.8: A and B beads gather into alternating domains and the segregation s rises over tens of τ. The chains stretch slightly as the blocks are pulled apart (watch Rg). Heat back and the domains dissolve. For short chains in 2D the order–disorder transition is smeared by fluctuations, so expect a gradual change rather than a sharp one.

s counts A–A, B–B and A–B contacts between different chains (within 1.5σ) and compares the like-contact fraction with that of an ideal mixture: 0 means fully mixed, 1 fully separated. Try fA = 0.25 for small A domains in a B matrix.

Particles and potentials

Two liquids, S and C, of identical size. Like pairs attract with ε = 1, unlike pairs with εSC = 0.3. In the regular-solution picture the mixture separates below a critical temperature that grows with the mismatch 1 − εSC.

There are no molecules here, only atoms, so this is the simplest example of a phase transition driven by energy against mixing entropy.

What to look for

Cool from 2.0 to 0.8: S-rich and C-rich domains appear and slowly coarsen. The demixing s rises from about 0.05 to about 0.3 within 50 τ and keeps rising slowly, because coarsening in 2D is slow. At 50% C the domains should form interconnected stripes (spinodal decomposition); at 15–20% C, isolated droplets instead. Compare the Langevin and Bussi thermostats to see how momentum transport affects coarsening.

The domain width is estimated from the fraction of beads touching the other species: width ≈ 2 × bead spacing / interface fraction. It is a rough guide, not a structure-factor measurement.

Particles and potentials

An HP copolymer: a flexible chain of hydrophobic (H) and polar (P) beads in solvent. Only H–H pairs attract (εHH = 3.0); everything else is purely repulsive. This is an off-lattice version of the Lau–Dill HP model, the simplest model in which a sequence determines a folded structure. The H–H attraction is direct and temperature-independent, so folding here is driven by energy, and the chain folds on cooling. Real hydrophobic folding in water is largely entropy-driven, which this solvent cannot represent.

The default sequence HPHPPHHPHPPHPHHPPHPH is a common 20-mer test sequence for the 2D HP model. Edit the sequence or pick a preset to compare how well different sequences fold.

What to look for

Start at T* = 2.3: the chain is an open coil with almost no buried H beads. Cool to 1.0: it collapses and folds with its H beads in a core, and about 80% of H beads end up buried within tens of τ. The crossover is near T* ≈ 2. Folding of a single chain is stochastic: some runs find the fold quickly, others stay trapped for a while, and a fold can partly reopen. Watch the trend in the plot rather than single values. Longer sequences such as the 36-mer fold much more slowly.

Add more chains to see misfolding and aggregation: H beads stick to other chains instead of forming their own core, and the interchain H–H count grows.

Particles and potentials

Flexible P chains (20 beads, 5% of beads) in an athermal solvent. P–P pairs attract with εPP = 1.0 (rc = 2.5σ); P–S and S–S pairs are purely repulsive, so the solvent is poor for the polymer.

The attraction is direct and does not depend on temperature. Its effect is set by εPP/kBT: weak when hot, so chains are swollen coils; strong when cold, so they collapse. This is upper-critical-solution (UCST) behaviour, the standard poor-solvent case of polymer physics. The solvent here has no structure, so this model cannot produce the opposite, entropy-driven LCST behaviour; that needs a structured solvent such as the MB water system.

What to look for

Start at T* = 2.2: chains are swollen coils (Rg ≈ 3.4σ, self-contact ≈ 0.36). Cool to 0.6: they collapse into compact globules (Rg ≈ 2.6σ, self-contact ≈ 0.59) within tens of τ, the coil-to-globule transition below the θ temperature. Lowering εPP acts like heating.

Raise the concentration to 20% or more and cooling makes chains stick together into a polymer-rich phase instead. Each chain then keeps roughly its size, so watch the self-contact fraction rather than Rg.

Particles and potentials

A polymer brush: chains of 10 beads grafted on a flat substrate (bottom), 1.6σ apart by default (30 chains), with their first bead fixed. Chains are semiflexible (kbend = 1.0) and attract each other weakly (ε = 0.3). The last two beads of every chain are water-repelling (orange, ε = 0.2 with the liquid); the rest are water-loving (teal, ε = 1.2). The box is periodic sideways and empty above the droplet.

The droplet is a non-volatile model liquid of flexible 4-bead oligomers (ε = 1.0 between liquid beads), standing in for water. It evaporates very little and its own properties barely change over T* = 0.5–0.9, so changes in wetting come from the surface. The contact angle is estimated from the droplet's height h and footprint half-width a as θ = 2·atan(h/a), the circular-cap relation.

What to look for

Cold (T* = 0.5): the brush is ordered, its water-repelling ends form the top layer, and the droplet beads up (θ ≈ 144°, footprint ≈ 14σ). Heat to 0.7: the chain ends fluctuate and fold, exposing water-loving middle beads, and the droplet spreads (θ ≈ 124°, footprint ≈ 24σ).

Swap the chemistry (water-loving ends): θ ≈ 94° when cold, rising to about 106° at 0.7. A control with identical beads (both attractions 1.2) drifts from 98° to 103°, so a few degrees of any change come from the liquid and brush themselves; the chemistry effect is the difference from that drift. Single readings scatter by up to about 10°: the droplet is small, so watch the trend in the plot.

The brush-bead attraction εCC (default 0.3) sets how strongly chains stick to each other. Stronger attraction should pack the brush and hold the ends in place, weakening the temperature response; weaker attraction lets the chains fluctuate more. Only the default has been calibrated.

A few liquid molecules evaporate: about 3% of the droplet at 0.7 and 6% at 0.9. That is the liquid's real vapour pressure. A more cohesive liquid evaporates less, but then the cold droplet becomes too sluggish to bead up in reasonable time.

Sparser grafting reverses the trend. At 2.5σ the angle rises on heating (about 125° to 135°) even though contacts with middle beads grow from 5 to 39. Hardly any liquid enters the brush (under 4%) and none reaches the substrate. The likely cause is roughness: the hot sparse chains tilt and bunch, and the droplet partly bridges the gaps (a Cassie-like state), which raises the apparent angle. Around 1.6σ the brush is dense enough to stay smooth.

Particles and potentials

Mercedes-Benz (MB) water (Ben-Naim 1971; Silverstein, Haymet and Dill 1998): each water is a disk with three hydrogen-bonding arms 120° apart. Two waters form a hydrogen bond when they are about one unit apart and an arm of each points at the other.

U_HB = −ε_HB G(r − 1) Σ_k G(i_k·u − 1) Σ_l G(j_l·u + 1) G(x) = exp(−x²/2σ²), σ = 0.085 U_LJ: ε = 0.1, σ = 0.7 (water) solutes: σ = 1.5, purely repulsive (WCA) with water, ε = 0.1 with each other

Units here are those of the MB model: lengths in units of the hydrogen-bond distance and temperature as kBT/εHB, so the useful range is T* ≈ 0.12–0.40. Waters rotate as well as translate; the thermostat acts on both. Number density is 0.85 and the time step 0.01.

What to look for

The water network: about 2.5 hydrogen bonds per water at T* = 0.14 (close to the ice-like maximum of 3), 1.7 at 0.20, 1.1 at 0.26 and 0.8 at 0.34. Watch the arms line up into hexagonal patches when cold.

60 nonpolar solutes with almost no attraction for each other and none for water. Any clustering beyond chance comes from the water: joining up lets it rebuild the hydrogen bonds the solutes break. At T* = 0.22 about 48% of solutes sit in clusters of 3 or more; switch hydrogen bonds off (plain LJ water) and it drops to about 23%. Clustered solutes are highlighted.

The effect peaks at intermediate temperature: about 41% clustered at T* = 0.16, 48% at 0.22 and 33% at 0.32. When cold, the hydrophobic effect is entropic and weakens on cooling; when hot, the network that drives it melts. Expect doubled association, not a sharp collapse: all-or-nothing hydrophobic collapse needs nonpolar objects much larger than water's structure. A chain of the same beads (chain length above 1) has not been calibrated.

NVT dynamics

Equations of motion are integrated with time step Δt = 0.005 in reduced LJ units, with periodic boundaries and cell lists. Two thermostats are available for comparison.

Langevin m dv = F dt − γ m v dt + √(2γ m k_B T) dW Bussi K ← stochastic rescale toward ½ N_f k_B T

Langevin damps momentum locally, so it slows diffusion and removes hydrodynamics as γ grows. Bussi rescaling preserves momentum and leaves the Newtonian dynamics nearly intact, so solvent flow survives. Chains start on a lattice and are briefly disordered before each run.
