Energy vs disorder: find the melting point

Why does heat break structures? Sticking together lowers the energy, so cold molecules build structures. Breaking free increases disorder (entropy): there are many more ways to be scattered than to be neatly packed. Heat makes disorder more valuable, so at some temperature disorder wins and the structure falls apart. Press Heating scan: the computer starts from the ordered structure, warms it up slowly and measures the energy at every temperature. The four graphs then show where the melting point is.

Tip: after heating, press Cooling scan to watch the structure form again on the way down.

What is being simulated

Particles and potentials

Each amphiphile is an A–B dumbbell held by a harmonic bond. Every bead feels a Lennard-Jones potential, cut and shifted to zero at rc.

U_LJ(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶] − U_LJ(r_c) U_bond(r) = ½ k (r − r₀)², k = 200

Only the tail–tail pair (and optionally head–solvent) keeps the attractive well, with rc = 2.5σ. All other pairs are purely repulsive (WCA, rc = 21/6σ). The solvophobic drive is the B–B attraction against an athermal solvent. Number density is 0.6 in every system.

What to look for

Starts from five bilayers (a lamellar stack, 48% of beads in dumbbells) at T* = 0.5; with equal head and tail sizes (σA = 1.0) they stay intact. Heat to 2.0: they break up within 5–15 τ (aggregated fraction 99% → about 30%, largest cluster 108 → about 10 molecules). Set σA to 1.2 and rebuild: the bilayers fragment even when cold, because cone-shaped molecules prefer curved micelles (the packing-parameter argument).

From a disordered start (Box and view → Initial state) at T* = 2.0, molecules are dispersed. Cool to 0.5: tails find each other and small micelles grow over tens of τ. Heat back to 2.0: micelles fall apart within a few τ. Breakup is fast and assembly is slow, because assembly needs diffusion and collisions.

At fixed T* = 0.5, lower the concentration to about 8%: most molecules stay free. Raise it to 50%: most join micelles. Ideal micelle theory says the free-dumbbell density levels off near the critical micelle concentration; allow 100 τ or more per concentration to test that. Lowering εBB acts like heating. A micelle is any cluster of at least 4 molecules whose tails are within 1.5σ.

Particles and potentials

Each chain is Nc P beads (3–40, 12 by default) joined by harmonic bonds, with a bending penalty that favours straight chains. P–P pairs attract with well depth εPP (rc = 2.5σ). P–S and S–S pairs are purely repulsive, so the solvent is poor for the polymer.

U_bend(α) = k_bend (1 − cos α) α = angle between consecutive bonds

Defaults εPP = 1.75 and kbend = 6.4 were calibrated for Nc = 12 at 40% polymer; other lengths shift the crossover.

What to look for

Starts from a crystal slab of straight, hexagonally packed chains at T* = 1.2: about 85% crystalline, still about 80% after 50 τ. Heat to 2.0: it drops to about 25% within 5 τ but a remnant survives; at 2.5 it melts completely (3% after 30 τ). The crystal melts at a higher temperature than it forms on cooling: hysteresis.

From a disordered start at T* = 2.2 the chains form a polymer liquid. Cool to 1.2: within tens of τ, segments align side by side into small hexagonally packed crystallites (gold). Heat to 2.0: they melt within about 10 τ. Crystallinity stays partial (about 20% after 75 τ at 40% polymer, about 30% at 80%), as in real semicrystalline polymers.

A bead is crystalline when its six-fold order |ψ6| exceeds 0.6 and agrees in phase with at least 4 polymer neighbours within 1.45σ, inside a crystallite of 10 or more beads.

Particles and potentials

Each chain is a block of A beads followed by a block of B beads (Nc = 16, fA = 0.5 by default). All pairs attract with ε = 1, so the solvent is equally good for both blocks, except A–B, which attracts more weakly (εAB = 0.2). The missing A–B attraction is the incompatibility that χ describes in Flory–Huggins theory, with χ ∝ (1 − εAB)/T.

Because the blocks are bonded, they cannot separate into two large phases. They form domains about one chain size wide instead: microphase separation.

What to look for

Starts from lamellae, A and B blocks in alternating bands with the chains anchored at the interfaces, at T* = 0.8 (s ≈ 0.9, still 0.87 after 50 τ). Heat to 2.2: the bands mix slowly (s about 0.5 after 50 τ), because whole chains have to diffuse.

From a disordered start, cool from 2.2 to 0.8: A and B beads gather into alternating domains and the segregation s rises over tens of τ. The chains stretch slightly as the blocks are pulled apart (watch Rg). Heat back and the domains dissolve. For short chains in 2D the order–disorder transition is smeared by fluctuations, so expect a gradual change rather than a sharp one.

s counts A–A, B–B and A–B contacts between different chains (within 1.5σ) and compares the like-contact fraction with that of an ideal mixture: 0 means fully mixed, 1 fully separated. Try fA = 0.25 for small A domains in a B matrix.

Particles and potentials

Two liquids, S and C, of identical size. Like pairs attract with ε = 1, unlike pairs with εSC = 0.3. In the regular-solution picture the mixture separates below a critical temperature that grows with the mismatch 1 − εSC.

There are no molecules here, only atoms, so this is the simplest example of a phase transition driven by energy against mixing entropy.

What to look for

Starts from four stripes of liquid C at T* = 0.8 (s ≈ 0.8). Heat to 2.0: the stripes dissolve (s about 0.3 after 15 τ, 0.1 after 50 τ). Kept cold, the stripes stay but their interfaces roughen (s about 0.6).

From a disordered start, cool from 2.0 to 0.8: S-rich and C-rich domains appear and slowly coarsen. The demixing s rises from about 0.05 to about 0.3 within 50 τ and keeps rising slowly, because coarsening in 2D is slow. At 50% C the domains should form interconnected stripes (spinodal decomposition); at 15–20% C, isolated droplets instead. Compare the Langevin and Bussi thermostats to see how momentum transport affects coarsening.

The domain width is estimated from the fraction of beads touching the other species: width ≈ 2 × bead spacing / interface fraction. It is a rough guide, not a structure-factor measurement.

Particles and potentials

An HP copolymer: a flexible chain of hydrophobic (H) and polar (P) beads in solvent. Only H–H pairs attract (εHH = 3.0); everything else is purely repulsive. This is an off-lattice version of the Lau–Dill HP model, the simplest model in which a sequence determines a folded structure. The H–H attraction is direct and temperature-independent, so folding here is driven by energy, and the chain folds on cooling. Real hydrophobic folding in water is largely entropy-driven, which this solvent cannot represent.

The default sequence HPHPPHHPHPPHPHHPPHPH is a common 20-mer test sequence for the 2D HP model. Edit the sequence or pick a preset to compare how well different sequences fold.

What to look for

Starts from the folded chain at T* = 1.0. For the preset sequences the fold comes from simulated annealing; a custom sequence starts as a compact block. Heat to 2.3: the hydrophobic core loosens (buried H from about 0.6–0.9 to 0.1–0.5, noisy), but the chain stays compact for a long time: in this dense solvent a 20-bead chain needs more than 100 τ to swell into an open coil, even with no attraction at all.

From a disordered start at T* = 2.3 the chain is an open coil with almost no buried H beads. Cool to 1.0: it collapses and folds with its H beads in a core, and about 80% of H beads end up buried within tens of τ. The crossover is near T* ≈ 2. Folding of a single chain is stochastic: some runs find the fold quickly, others stay trapped for a while, and a fold can partly reopen. Watch the trend in the plot rather than single values. Longer sequences such as the 36-mer fold much more slowly.

Add more chains to see misfolding and aggregation: H beads stick to other chains instead of forming their own core, and the interchain H–H count grows.

Particles and potentials

Flexible P chains (20 beads, 5% of beads) in an athermal solvent. P–P pairs attract with εPP = 1.0 (rc = 2.5σ); P–S and S–S pairs are purely repulsive, so the solvent is poor for the polymer.

The attraction is direct and does not depend on temperature. Its effect is set by εPP/kBT: weak when hot, so chains are swollen coils; strong when cold, so they collapse. This is upper-critical-solution (UCST) behaviour, the standard poor-solvent case of polymer physics. The solvent here has no structure, so this model cannot produce the opposite, entropy-driven LCST behaviour; that needs a structured solvent such as the MB water system.

What to look for

Starts from collapsed globules at T* = 0.6. Heat to 2.2: they swell within about 15 τ (Rg 1.9σ → 2.9σ, self-contact 0.76 → 0.47).

From a disordered start at T* = 2.2 the chains are swollen coils (Rg ≈ 3.4σ, self-contact ≈ 0.36). Cool to 0.6: they collapse into compact globules (Rg ≈ 2.6σ, self-contact ≈ 0.59) within tens of τ, the coil-to-globule transition below the θ temperature. Lowering εPP acts like heating.

Raise the concentration to 20% or more and cooling makes chains stick together into a polymer-rich phase instead. Each chain then keeps roughly its size, so watch the self-contact fraction rather than Rg.

Particles and potentials

A polymer brush: chains of 10 beads grafted on a flat substrate (bottom), 1.6σ apart by default (30 chains in the default 48σ box), with their first bead fixed. The box size setting gives 36σ, 48σ or 64σ, and the droplet is scaled to match (270, 480 or about 850 beads). Chains are semiflexible (kbend = 1.0) and attract each other weakly (ε = 0.3). The last two beads of every chain are water-repelling (orange, ε = 0.2 with the liquid); the rest are water-loving (teal, ε = 1.2). The box is periodic sideways and empty above the droplet.

The droplet is a non-volatile model liquid of flexible 4-bead oligomers (ε = 1.0 between liquid beads), standing in for water. It evaporates much less than MB water would. The contact angle is estimated from the droplet's height h and footprint half-width a as θ = 2·atan(h/a), the circular-cap relation.

What to look for

Starts with the brush upright at T* = 0.5 and the droplet already beaded into a cap (about 120°).

Watch which beads the liquid touches (plot and "Liquid contacts" readout). Cold (T* = 0.5): the brush is ordered and its orange ends form the top layer, so the liquid touches almost only orange beads (teal contacts about 0–3). Heat to 0.7–0.9: the chain ends fluctuate and fold, and liquid contacts with the teal middle beads rise, but modestly and very unevenly between runs: from about 1–3 to 3–7 in most runs, up to about 20 in some. Swap the colours and the opposite happens: the liquid starts on teal ends, and heating replaces part of those contacts with orange middles.

The contact angle is shown but should not be over-read. For a droplet this small it fluctuates by about ±20° between runs, and in repeated runs (three seeds, 120 τ each) we could not resolve a systematic temperature change of the angle at the default settings: about 119° at both T* = 0.5 and 0.7. The change in surface chemistry is real; turning it into a measurable change of the angle would need a larger droplet or a stronger chemistry contrast.

The liquid has a real vapour pressure, so a few molecules evaporate when hot; gravity (default g = 0.003) pulls the droplet onto the brush. A more cohesive liquid evaporates less, but it also becomes too sluggish to reshape when cold. Sparser grafting (2σ and above) lets chains tilt and bunch, which makes the surface rough.

Particles and potentials

Mercedes-Benz (MB) water (Ben-Naim 1971; Silverstein, Haymet and Dill 1998): each water is a disk with three hydrogen-bonding arms 120° apart. Two waters form a hydrogen bond when they are about one unit apart and an arm of each points at the other.

U_HB = −ε_HB G(r − 1) Σ_k G(i_k·u − 1) Σ_l G(j_l·u + 1) G(x) = exp(−x²/2σ²), σ = 0.085 U_LJ: ε = 0.1, σ = 0.7 (water) solutes: σ = 1.5, purely repulsive (WCA) with water, ε = 0.1 with each other

Units here are those of the MB model: lengths in units of the hydrogen-bond distance and temperature as kBT/εHB, so the useful range is T* ≈ 0.12–0.40. Waters rotate as well as translate; the thermostat acts on both. Number density is 0.85 and the time step 0.01.

What to look for

Starts with 60% of the solutes in small clusters of 7 at T* = 0.22, where clustering stays around 50–58%. Heat to 0.34: it falls to about a third over 50 τ.

The water network: about 2.5 hydrogen bonds per water at T* = 0.14 (close to the ice-like maximum of 3), 1.7 at 0.20, 1.1 at 0.26 and 0.8 at 0.34. Watch the arms line up into hexagonal patches when cold.

60 nonpolar solutes with almost no attraction for each other and none for water. Any clustering beyond chance comes from the water: joining up lets it rebuild the hydrogen bonds the solutes break. At T* = 0.22 about 48% of solutes sit in clusters of 3 or more; switch hydrogen bonds off (plain LJ water) and it drops to about 23%. Clustered solutes are highlighted.

The effect peaks at intermediate temperature: about 41% clustered at T* = 0.16, 48% at 0.22 and 33% at 0.32. When cold, the hydrophobic effect is entropic and weakens on cooling; when hot, the network that drives it melts. Expect doubled association, not a sharp collapse: all-or-nothing hydrophobic collapse needs nonpolar objects much larger than water's structure. A chain of the same beads (chain length above 1) has not been calibrated.

What is simulated

836 water molecules in the Mercedes-Benz (MB) model, the same water as in "Chain in MB water". Each molecule is a disk with three arms 120° apart, like a simplified H2O that can make three hydrogen bonds. Two molecules bond when they are one unit apart and an arm of each points at the other.

The start is a perfect crystal of 2D ice: a honeycomb in which every molecule holds 3 hydrogen bonds. The box is the size of that crystal and stays fixed (constant N, V, T). A molecule counts as ice (blue) when it and all its close neighbours hold 3 hydrogen bonds.

Units are those of the MB model: lengths in hydrogen-bond distances, temperature as kBT/εHB.

What to look for

Cold (T* = 0.12): the honeycomb stays, about 80–95% ice, with molecules only wobbling. Around T* = 0.18 the crystal starts to fall apart from defects; at 0.22 it is fully liquid within about 20 τ: ice drops to 0%, and hydrogen bonds per molecule from 3 to about 1.4.

Cool it again and it does not come straight back: from the liquid, 200 τ at T* = 0.10–0.13 rebuilds only about 40% ice, as small crystals with defects between them. Freezing needs a nucleus to grow from, so a liquid can stay supercooled well below the melting point. The thermodynamics scan shows the same hysteresis.

The liquid has the same volume as the ice but is denser in its own right, so it opens small holes when the ice melts. Real ice floats for the same reason: its open hydrogen-bonded lattice takes more room than the liquid.

NVT dynamics

Equations of motion are integrated with time step Δt = 0.005 in reduced LJ units, with periodic boundaries and cell lists. Two thermostats are available for comparison. Each system starts from its ordered, cold-equilibrium structure, relaxed for 1 τ, so heating (or noise in the room) can destroy it right away; choose Disordered under Box and view to watch it assemble instead.

The thermodynamics scan ramps the temperature linearly and bins the energy by temperature. C = dU/dT is taken from the binned energies, S by integrating dS = dU/T, and F = U − TS. Straight-line fits of U below and above the peak give the two phase branches, extrapolated across the transition; where their free energies cross is the transition estimate. Because the ramp is finite, a heating scan overshoots and a cooling scan undershoots (hysteresis). Single or dilute chains (HP copolymer, UCST polymer at low concentration) give only a smooth crossover with no resolvable peak, and in MB water the dominant peak near T* = 0.2 is the melting of the water's own hydrogen-bond network.

Langevin m dv = F dt − γ m v dt + √(2γ m k_B T) dW Bussi K ← stochastic rescale toward ½ N_f k_B T

Langevin damps momentum locally, so it slows diffusion and removes hydrodynamics as γ grows. Bussi rescaling preserves momentum and leaves the Newtonian dynamics nearly intact, so solvent flow survives.
