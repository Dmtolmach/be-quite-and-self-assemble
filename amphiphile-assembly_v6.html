What do the graphs mean?

Why does heat break structures? Sticking together lowers the energy, so cold molecules build structures. Breaking free increases disorder (entropy): there are many more ways to be scattered than to be neatly packed. Heat makes disorder more valuable, so at some temperature disorder wins and the structure falls apart.

1 · Energy U: how much energy the molecules hold. It climbs as the structure breaks, because broken contacts cost energy. At constant volume it plays the role of the enthalpy H.

2 · Heat capacity C: how much heat it takes to warm up a little. The peak is the melting point: there, heat goes into breaking the structure instead of warming it.

3 · Entropy S: the amount of disorder (how many ways the molecules can be arranged). It jumps up when the structure breaks.

4 · Free energy F = U − T·S: energy minus the “disorder bonus”. Nature picks the state with the lower F. Where the two dashed lines cross, ordered and broken are equally good: that is the melting point.

In everyday life

Bile from your liver is full of amphiphiles. They break the fat in your food into tiny droplets, so that it can be digested.

Every cell in your body is wrapped in a bilayer of amphiphiles (lipids), just like the starting structure here. Bacteria and fish change the make-up of their lipids with the seasons so that their membranes neither melt in warm water nor freeze stiff in cold water.

Milk is tiny droplets of fat in water, each wrapped in a thin skin of amphiphilic molecules that stops the droplets from merging.

Most plastics are partly crystalline polymers, like the gold patches here. A milk bottle (polyethylene) looks milky because light scatters off its tiny crystallites; heat it and it turns clear and soft as the crystals melt.

Candle wax is made of short chains that crystallize the same way: liquid when hot, cloudy and solid when it cools.

Ouzo and pastis turn cloudy when water is added: the anise oil they contain no longer mixes and separates into countless tiny droplets.

Some liquids mix only when warm: water and phenol become one liquid above about 66 °C and split into two layers again when cooled. Metal alloys and glasses can also separate into two phases while they cool, which chemists use to make porous glass.

Rubber gloves swell in some solvents because the solvent is good for the polymer, and a polymer dissolved in a poor solvent comes out of solution when cooled, just as the chains here collapse on cooling.

The opposite behaviour exists in water: PNIPAM, a polymer used in smart gels, is dissolved below 32 °C and collapses above it. That needs the special structure of water, which this model does not have.

Drops of oil floating on soup join into bigger drops: the water pushes oily molecules together so that it can keep its own hydrogen bonds. The same push folds proteins and holds cell membranes together.

Some proteins unfold not only when heated but also when cooled far enough (cold denaturation), a sign that the hydrophobic effect weakens in the cold, as it does here.

Frying meat and heating milk: heat unfolds the proteins, their hidden sticky parts stick to each other, the meat turns firm and a skin forms on the milk.

In the body, proteins that unfold and clump cause diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's. Cells guard against it with chaperone proteins that cover exposed sticky parts until the protein can fold again.

Frost on a window starts from scratches and specks of dust: freezing needs a starting point (a nucleus), and the crystals grow out from there.

Salt on icy roads melts the ice even below 0 °C: the salt dissolved in the water gets in the way of the molecules locking into ice.

Slow freezing makes large, clear ice crystals (as in clear ice cubes); fast freezing makes many small ones, which is why ice cream is churned while it freezes quickly: small crystals feel smooth.
